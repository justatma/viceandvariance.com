You Don’t Get to Decide What Your Brand Means

Why startup founders should discover their brand through customers, not impose it on them.

BY ATMA DEGEYNDT · 6 MIN READ ● 5 CITATIONS

I've worked with enough startup founders to recognize the pattern. A company is barely off the ground, and the founder is already preoccupied with the brand. They want to settle the logo, the colors, the voice, the personality, the story. What they don't have is a convincing account of why customers choose their product.

The work feels productive. There are decisions to make, agencies to brief, designs to approve. You can see progress. Customer discovery is less comfortable. People misunderstand the product. They don't care about the feature you're proudest of. They describe a benefit you never considered. They leave without buying.

And that's precisely the information a new company needs.

I'm not suggesting that startups operate without names, visual identities, or coherent messaging. People have to recognize you and understand what you're offering. The mistake is treating those early choices as the brand itself, then defending them before the market has had much of a say.

A founder can decide what a brand is supposed to mean. Customers decide what it actually means.

01

Identity is what you make. Meaning is what they take away.

There are three separate things that startup founders routinely lump together as branding. Identity is what the company chooses: its name, design, language, and public presentation. Positioning is the claim it wants to occupy in the customer's mind. Brand meaning is the set of associations people actually form through advertising, recommendations, prices, product use, and whatever happens when something goes wrong.

Those things influence one another. They are not interchangeable.

Marketing scholar Kevin Lane Keller made customer associations central to his account of brand equity. In his model, what matters is not simply what a company communicates, but what people know, remember, and associate with the brand, and how that changes their response to its marketing.1 A company can work to shape those associations. It cannot guarantee them by putting the right adjectives in a presentation.

Experience matters here as well. Research by J. Joško Brakus, Bernd Schmitt, and Lia Zarantonello identifies sensory, emotional, intellectual, and behavioral dimensions of brand experience and connects those experiences with satisfaction and loyalty.2 That's a more useful way to think about resonance than asking a founder to choose whether a new brand should feel 'bold' or 'authentic.'

The customer encounters the actual business, not the brand guide. The checkout that works. The support email nobody answers. The unexpected detail that makes the product useful. The promise the product keeps, or doesn't.

You can announce that you're trustworthy. You cannot announce that customers trust you.

02

Premature branding makes assumptions expensive to abandon

This is why I worry less about what founders spend on an early logo than about what happens after they approve it. Every round of decisions increases their attachment to a particular story. Soon the product is for 'ambitious disruptors' or 'people who refuse to settle.' The company begins hiring, writing copy, and selecting audiences to fit a personality the founder invented before enough customers had used the thing.

Now imagine those customers respond differently. They don't want disruption. They want something dependable that gives them one less problem to deal with on Tuesday morning.

A founder who regards the original positioning as a working hypothesis can make use of that discovery. A founder who regards it as part of the company's identity may dismiss those customers as the wrong audience, or decide that marketing hasn't explained the vision clearly enough.

Psychology gives us two reasons this happens. Confirmation bias inclines people to notice and interpret information in ways that support what they already believe.3 Sunk-cost effects make it harder to walk away from decisions once time, money, and effort have gone into them.4 These are general findings about human judgment, not special defects unique to entrepreneurs. Founders simply have unusually personal reasons to hold on to a story they created.

A polished brand can become a sophisticated excuse not to learn.

03

The customer may be buying something else entirely

Imagine a startup selling invoicing software to independent professionals. The founder believes the product's value is speed. The early positioning promises more efficiency, faster workflows, and more time to do the work customers love. It's a reasonable hypothesis. The product does send invoices quickly.

Then the company begins talking with people who use it repeatedly. A different benefit comes up. These customers dread asking clients for money. They worry about sounding aggressive. They lose track of overdue bills. They want a professional way to follow up without initiating another awkward conversation.

Speed may be useful, but the emotional payoff is relief. The product helps customers feel more in control of an uncomfortable part of running a business.

This is a hypothetical example, but the distinction is real. The feature a founder is proud of isn't necessarily the benefit customers remember. And the benefit they remember may reveal a more specific group of people worth serving.

Now the company has something to test. Does a message about getting paid without uncomfortable follow-ups attract better-qualified buyers than a message about faster invoicing? Do people who care about that problem activate, pay, stay, and recommend the product? Are automatic reminders genuinely the feature they rely on, or just the easiest thing to mention in an interview?

If the evidence holds, the work extends beyond the headline on the website. The product team may prioritize reminder controls, polite templates, and clear payment status. Customer support may teach users how to handle difficult payment conversations. The company may discover that certain freelancers respond far more strongly than businesses with dedicated accounts-receivable staff.

That is brand development. The company is learning what it means to customers and deciding how to become better at delivering it.

04

Build a brand you can still learn from

A startup needs enough identity to be legible and consistent. Give the company a serviceable name. Make the website clear. Be recognizable across the places customers encounter you. Then keep the expensive declarations provisional.

Begin with a claim you can test. Who is this for? What problem do they believe they're solving? What alternatives are they using now? Why would they choose this product, and what would disappoint them after buying it? Steve Blank's customer-development approach treats early business ideas as hypotheses to investigate with real customers rather than assumptions to protect.5 Brand positioning deserves the same treatment.

Ask people to describe the product before they see your carefully written explanation. Listen for the words they choose without prompting. Ask what they expected, what surprised them, and how they'd explain it to a friend. Speak with people who tried it once and never returned, not just the people who tell you they love it.

Then look at behavior. What message brings in customers who actually buy? Which reasons for buying are associated with repeat use? What do people recommend to others without being asked? A beautiful interview quote can be illuminating, but it isn't a substitute for a purchase, a renewal, or a referral.

Test competing positions with comparable audiences and conditions. Compare more than clicks. If one message attracts curiosity and another attracts customers who stay, that difference matters. If a promised benefit drives signups but disappoints people after use, the brand is learning the wrong lesson from its own acquisition numbers.

None of this requires founders to redesign their identity every week. In fact, constant changes can make a company harder to recognize. Keep enough consistency for customers to remember you. Change what the evidence shows is wrong. Preserve what customers have come to value.

05

The company changes, too

Customer discovery can change the company, not just its advertising. You don't just discover what people think of the product. You discover which people are finding something distinctive in it, and what kind of company you need to become to serve them well.

Not every reaction deserves equal weight. One vocal customer shouldn't dictate your positioning. Nor should a founder chase whichever segment delivers the cheapest click this week. Look for recurring patterns among customers who get real value, return, and bring others with similar needs. Those patterns help define an audience worth choosing.

As that audience develops, the relationship runs both ways. The company learns what customers care about. Customers learn what they can expect from the company. Product choices reinforce some associations and weaken others. Over time, a distinctive brand can develop from that repeated exchange.

Founders often want to establish a brand before they establish that relationship. They settle on a story, then start treating customer feedback as a test of how well they told it.

Choose an identity. Make a promise you can test. Put the product into people's hands. Pay attention to what they find valuable, especially when it's different from what you expected. The brand you eventually build should reflect what you discovered, not what you decided on day one.
